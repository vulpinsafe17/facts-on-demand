# Where Did Chess Really Come From? — voice-over script (EN)

Target: about 15 minutes at Eddie's pace (~150 words per minute, film 1 measured). Chapter headings are not read aloud.
Every fact is listed with two sources in `research.md`. Lines marked as legend are told as legend on screen too.

## 1. Hook

For almost a thousand years, the piece standing next to the king was one of the weakest on the chessboard. It could move one square at a time. Diagonally. That's it.
Then, in fifteenth-century Spain, that piece, by now a queen, suddenly became the most powerful piece in the game. Overnight, chess got faster, wilder, and a lot more dangerous.
Today, hundreds of millions of people play this game. Kings banned it. A fake robot used it to fool an emperor. And a computer used it to beat the best player on Earth.
But where did chess actually come from? Who invented it?
Let's find out.

## 2. India: an army on a board

Our story starts in northern India, about fifteen hundred years ago.
The earliest clear evidence of the game comes from around the sixth century, in the time of the Gupta Empire and the kingdoms that followed it.
The game was called chaturanga. In Sanskrit, that means "four limbs". Or, in old poetry, simply: "an army".
And that's exactly what it was. An army on a board. Four divisions, just like a real Indian army of the time: infantry, cavalry, elephants, and chariots.
Look closely, and you can already see our modern pieces. The foot soldiers became pawns. The cavalry became knights. The elephants would one day become bishops. And the chariots? They became rooks.
Next to the king stood his advisor, the minister. Not a queen. There was no queen yet. Remember that. It matters later.
Nobody knows exactly who invented chaturanga. But there's a famous legend that tries to answer it anyway.

## 3. The legend of the wheat

The story goes like this. A wise man invents chess and shows it to his king. The king loves it so much that he offers the inventor any reward he wants.
The wise man asks for something that sounds almost insulting. One grain of wheat on the first square of the board. Two grains on the second. Four on the third. Doubling every time, all the way to square sixty-four.
The king laughs. That's it? Some wheat? Done.
Then his servants start counting.
By square twenty, it's already more than half a million grains on that one square. By the last square, the total comes to eighteen quintillion grains. That's eighteen, followed by eighteen zeros.
That's about one point two trillion tonnes of wheat. More than a thousand years of the entire world's wheat harvest today.
The king had promised something he could never pay.
Now, this is a legend. The oldest known written version comes from a scholar named Ibn Khallikan, in the year 1256. That's seven hundred years after chess appeared. But the maths is real. And it's a great way to learn that you should never let a mathematician pick his own reward.

## 4. Persia: the king is helpless

From India, the game travelled west, to the Persian Empire. There, the Persians called it chatrang.
And they had their own story about how it arrived.
According to a book written in Middle Persian, an Indian king sent a gift to the great Persian ruler Khosrow the First, who reigned from 531 to 579.
The gift was a chess set. And a challenge. Figure out how this game works, or pay us tribute.
Nobody at the Persian court had a clue. Except one man: the wise minister Bozorgmehr. He worked out the rules, beat the Indian envoy at his own game, and then struck back. He invented a brand-new game and sent it to India as a counter-challenge.
That game was backgammon. And this time, it was the Indian scholars who couldn't solve it.
Is the story true? Probably not, at least not word for word. But it tells us that chess was important enough in Persia to become a matter of national pride.
And the Persians left something behind that we still use today. When a king was trapped and could not escape, the player said: shah mat. "The king is helpless."
Say it fast enough, a few hundred times, across a few hundred years, and you get a word you already know.
Checkmate.

## 5. Baghdad: the masters with books

In the seventh century, Arab armies conquered Persia. They didn't just take the land. They took the game, too.
In Arabic, chatrang became shatranj. And when the Abbasid caliphs took power in 750, chess became a passion in their capital, Baghdad.
This is where chess got its first real masters.
Around 840, a player named al-Adli was the best in the world. He wrote one of the very first books about the game. And then, in front of the caliph al-Mutawakkil, he lost a match to a younger player named ar-Razi.
A few decades later came the greatest of them all: al-Suli. He was a companion to three caliphs, and he was so good that his name stayed famous for centuries.
These masters gave the pieces values, ranked players by strength, and wrote down puzzles that people still study.
But the game was slow. Very slow. The minister, now called the firzan, could only step one square diagonally. The elephant could only jump two squares diagonally. A single game could take hours just to get the pieces into battle.

## 6. Europe: the board becomes a royal court

From the Islamic world, chess spread along the trade routes. Through Spain. Through Sicily and Italy. Along the rivers into Russia.
By around the year 1000, it had reached much of Europe.
And here, something interesting happened. Europeans didn't really know what to do with an Indian war game. They had no war elephants. They had no viziers.
So they turned the army into something they did understand. A royal court.
The vizier became a queen. The elephant became a bishop in some countries, a fool or a jester in others. And the chariot became a castle tower.
The oldest known European text about chess is a Latin poem from a monastery in Einsiedeln, in today's Switzerland, written around the year 1000. And it's also the oldest text we know of that mentions the chess queen.
But don't get excited. She was still the same weak piece. New crown. Same tiny steps.

## 7. The Lewis chessmen

Now let's jump to a beach in Scotland. The year is 1831.
On the Isle of Lewis, in the Outer Hebrides, someone digs up a hidden hoard. Inside are ninety-three little figures, carved mostly from walrus ivory.
Kings. Queens. Bishops. Knights on tiny horses. And some very angry warriors, biting the tops of their shields.
These are the Lewis chessmen. They were made around eight hundred and fifty years ago, most likely in Norway, probably in the city of Trondheim. Back then, the islands belonged to the Kingdom of Norway, not Scotland.
Who buried them, and why? Nobody knows.
Today, most of them are in the British Museum in London, and the rest are in the National Museum of Scotland in Edinburgh. They are probably the most famous chess pieces in the world.

## 8. Kings against chess

By now, chess was the game of kings. Literally.
In 1283, King Alfonso the Tenth of Castile, a ruler so learned that history calls him Alfonso the Wise, finished a huge illustrated book of games. It has more than a hundred chess problems in it. It's the oldest European book of its kind that we still have.
But not every king was a fan.
Medieval Europeans loved to play chess for money. A lot of money. So in 1254, King Louis the Ninth of France, a king so pious that he was later made a saint, issued an ordinance against gambling. And chess was caught up in the crackdown.
Churchmen weren't fans either. In the eleventh century, a cardinal named Peter Damian scolded a bishop for playing chess at all.
Sound familiar? Once again, the people in power tried to stop a game.
And once again, the game simply kept going.

## 9. Spain, 1475: the queen goes wild

Now, remember that weak little queen?
Around 1475, in the city of Valencia, in Spain, three poets wrote a poem called Scachs d'amor. "The Chess of Love."
In it, they describe a game of chess. But a very different kind of chess. In this game, the queen can move as far as she wants. In any direction. Straight lines. Diagonals. Across the entire board.
The bishop got a boost too. Instead of hopping two squares, it could now slide along its whole diagonal.
Suddenly, games that took hours could end in a handful of moves. Some people even called it "mad queen chess".
Why did this happen? Nobody knows for sure. One popular theory links it to the real Queen Isabella of Castile, one of the most powerful women in Europe at the time. Other historians say that's a nice story without much proof.
Either way, the new rules spread across Europe in just a few decades.
And that was it. Almost a thousand years after chaturanga, the chess we play today was finally born.

## 10. The Turk

Let's jump to Vienna, in 1770.
An inventor named Wolfgang von Kempelen unveils a sensation at the court of Empress Maria Theresa. A life-sized mechanical man, dressed in Ottoman robes, seated behind a wooden cabinet full of gears.
They called it the Turk. And it played chess. Really well.
For more than eighty years, the Turk toured Europe and America. It played Benjamin Franklin. And in 1809, it played Napoleon Bonaparte.
Napoleon, being Napoleon, tried to cheat. He made illegal moves. The Turk calmly put the pieces back. And when he kept going, the story says it swept all the pieces off the board.
So how did a machine from the seventeen hundreds play chess better than an emperor?
Simple. It didn't.
There was a strong human chess player hidden inside the cabinet the whole time. The gears were just for show.
The Turk ended its days forgotten in a museum in Philadelphia. In 1854, a fire destroyed it.
But keep this machine in mind. Because two hundred years later, the idea came back. And this time, it was real.

## 11. London: the pieces get their look

By the eighteen hundreds, chess had a problem. Every chess set looked different. Some pieces were so fancy that you could barely tell a bishop from a pawn.
So in 1849, a new design was registered in London. Simple, clear, and easy to tell apart. It was made by the company Jaques of London and named after the best English player of the day, Howard Staunton.
Today, it's called the Staunton set. And if you picture a chess set right now, that's almost certainly the one you see.
Two years later, in 1851, London hosted the first international chess tournament ever, during the Great Exhibition. Staunton organised it, and played in it too.
He didn't win. The winner was a German maths teacher named Adolf Anderssen. And on his way to the title, he beat Staunton himself.

## 12. The first world champion

There was still one question nobody could answer. Who was the best player in the world?
In 1886, two men decided to settle it. Wilhelm Steinitz and Johannes Zukertort played a long match across three American cities: New York, St. Louis, and New Orleans.
Zukertort raced ahead at the start. But Steinitz fought back and won.
He became the first official World Chess Champion.
Then, in 1924, chess got its own world organisation. It was founded in Paris and given a French name: FIDE. It still runs world chess today.

## 13. Reykjavik, 1972

For decades after the Second World War, one country owned chess. The Soviet Union. Champion after champion, all Soviet. For twenty-four years.
Then came 1972. The Cold War. And a match in Reykjavik, Iceland, that the whole world watched.
On one side of the board: the Soviet champion, Boris Spassky. On the other: a brilliant and very difficult American named Bobby Fischer.
Fischer arrived late. He made demands about almost everything. He even lost one game by simply not showing up.
And then he won the match.
For a few weeks, a board game felt like a battle between superpowers. And for a short time, the Soviet run was over.

## 14. Deep Blue

Remember the Turk? The fake chess machine with a human hiding inside?
In 1997, in New York, the real thing showed up. An IBM computer called Deep Blue played a six-game match against the world champion, Garry Kasparov.
A year earlier, Kasparov had beaten it.
This time, he didn't. On May 11th, 1997, Deep Blue won the final game, and the match.
For the first time, a computer had beaten the reigning world champion in a match under normal tournament conditions.
Today, the chess program on your phone could probably beat every human who has ever lived.
And yet, people play more than ever.

## 15. Ending

Why? Maybe because chess is bigger than any of us.
Back in 1950, the mathematician Claude Shannon estimated how many different games of chess are possible. His answer: at least ten to the power of one hundred and twenty.
That's a one with a hundred and twenty zeros. Far more than the number of atoms in the observable universe.
So, who invented chess?
Nobody, really. Indian players built the army. Persians gave it a king that could be helpless. Baghdad's masters wrote the first books. Europe crowned a queen. And poets in Valencia set her free.
Fifteen hundred years later, an estimated six hundred million people still play it.
Not bad for a game about a king who can only move one square at a time.
