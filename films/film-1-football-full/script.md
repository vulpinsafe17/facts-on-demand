# Where Did Football Really Come From? (full film, EN)
Narrator: Eddie Starling. One ElevenLabs take per chapter (## = chapter, blank line = topic pause).

## 0 Hook
In 1314, the King of England tried to ban a game.
It didn't work.
Neither did the next thirty bans.
Today, that game is played by more than 250 million people, in almost every country on Earth.
And yet, if you ask who actually invented it, you'll start an argument.
The Chinese say China. The English say England. Your uncle says his village.
So let's settle it, once and for all.
Where did football really come from?

## 1 China
Our story starts in China, more than two thousand years ago.
In the city of Linzi, people were obsessed with a game called cuju.
It literally means "kick ball." Simple name. Simple idea.
The ball? Leather panels, stitched together and stuffed with feathers.
Not exactly a World Cup match ball.
But the emperors loved it.
When the Han dynasty began, its first emperor, Gaozu, had a cuju court built right at his palace.
Generals used the game to train their soldiers. It kept them fit, fast and sharp.

Over the centuries, cuju turned into something surprisingly modern.
In the Song dynasty, there were cuju clubs, touring teams, and even professional players.
One man, Gao Qiu, was so good with a ball that the emperor himself took notice.
Emperor Huizong loved the game, and Gao Qiu rose all the way to Grand Marshal of the army.
Imagine getting promoted to general because of your keepy-uppies.

Then the mood changed.
The first Ming emperor, Hongwu, decided his officers were playing far too much and working far too little.
So he banned cuju. And according to some accounts, the punishment could include losing a foot.
Which, for a football player, is a very serious red card.

But here's the twist.
FIFA itself calls cuju one of the earliest ball games played with the feet.
And yet there is no evidence that it shaped the game we play today.
To find that connection, we have to keep looking.

## 2 The rest of the ancient world
On the other side of the world, the ancient Greeks played a ball game called episkyros.
Two teams, a line between them, and a lot of shouting.
But they mostly carried and threw the ball. So that's less football, more rugby.
The Romans borrowed the idea and called it harpastum. Same story. Hands everywhere.

In Japan, nobles played kemari.
A circle of players, a deerskin ball, and one goal: keep it in the air, together.
No winners. No losers. Very polite. Very un-football.
It is still performed today at shrines in Japan, in full court costume.

And in Central America, people played a ball game more than three thousand years ago, with a heavy rubber ball.
But they struck it with their hips, not their feet.
Impressive. Painful. Still not football.

## 3 Medieval England
So let's jump to medieval England, where things get loud.
Picture this. One village against the next.
Any number of players. One ball. And almost no rules.
The goals could be miles apart, and the pitch was everything in between.
Streets, fields, rivers, the market. Your front garden.
They called it mob football, and it was exactly as chaotic as it sounds.
In the town of Ashbourne, they still play it every year, with two goals three miles apart.

Then came April 1314.
The merchants of London had had enough of the noise, the broken stalls and the flying chickens.
So, in the name of King Edward the Second, football was banned in the city.
Did it work? Of course not.
His son, Edward the Third, banned it too. He wanted his men practising archery for war, not chasing a ball.
Scotland agreed. In 1424, King James the First declared that no man shall play at the foot ball.
Later Scottish kings banned golf as well, just to be fair.

Then there's Henry the Eighth.
In 1526, he ordered a pair of football boots for four shillings.
In 1540, he banned football anyway. Rules for thee, boots for me.
Even Shakespeare got involved. In King Lear, a servant is insulted as a "base football player."
Between 1314 and 1667, England passed more than thirty laws against football.
Football won every single time.

## 4 Schools and the first clubs
By the 1800s, football had moved into the great English schools.
But there was a huge problem. Every school played by its own rules.
In some, you could catch the ball. In others, you could run with it.
In some, you could kick your opponent in the shins. Yes, really.
And when two schools met, the first half of the match was basically an argument about the rules.

Legend says that at Rugby School, a boy simply picked up the ball and ran, and a new sport was born.
Historians aren't convinced. But the split between kicking and carrying was real.
In 1848, students at Cambridge University tried to write one set of rules for everyone.
It helped. A little.

Then, in 1857, a group of cricket players in Sheffield wanted something to keep them fit through the winter.
So they founded Sheffield Football Club, recognised by FIFA as the oldest football club in the world.
They needed someone to play against. So in 1860, they played Hallam, and that fixture is still played today.
The oldest derby in football history started because some cricketers were bored in winter.

## 5 1863: the rules
And then, on a Monday evening in October 1863, everything changed.
A solicitor named Ebenezer Cobb Morley thought football needed one set of rules, just like cricket had.
He wrote to a newspaper, and clubs from around London answered.
On the 26th of October, they met at the Freemasons' Tavern in London.
That night, they founded the Football Association.

Over six meetings, the rules took shape.
The biggest fight was about something called hacking. Kicking your opponent in the leg, on purpose.
One club, Blackheath, really wanted to keep it.
They argued that without it, the game would lose all its courage.
They lost. On the 8th of December 1863, thirteen laws were approved. No hacking. No running with the ball.
Blackheath walked out to keep playing their way.
And that's the moment football and rugby officially went their separate ways.
A few days later, the first match under the new rules ended nil nil. Of course it did.

## 6 Football goes big
From there, football grew up fast.
In 1872, the first FA Cup final was played in London. The Wanderers beat the Royal Engineers, one nil.
That same year, Scotland and England played the first ever international match, on a cricket ground in Glasgow.
Four thousand fans watched. The final score?
Nil nil. Some traditions start early.

Bit by bit, the game you know appeared.
A crossbar instead of a piece of tape.
In 1891, nets, invented by an engineer from Liverpool to end arguments about whether the ball went in.
And in the same year, the penalty kick, an idea from an Irish goalkeeper named William McCrum.
Players called it the kick of death.
Clubs started paying their players, and in 1888, twelve clubs formed the Football League.

Women played too, and huge crowds came to watch.
On Boxing Day 1920, fifty three thousand people packed into Goodison Park to watch Dick, Kerr Ladies.
The Football Association's reply? A ban.
In 1921, it declared football unsuitable for women, and kept them off its grounds for fifty years.
The ban was only lifted in 1971.

## 7 The world
Meanwhile, the British game was spreading across the planet, carried by sailors, engineers, teachers and students.
In 1904, seven nations met in Paris and founded FIFA.
France, Belgium, Denmark, the Netherlands, Spain, Sweden and Switzerland.
England, the country that wrote the rules, only joined the year after.
And in 1930, Uruguay hosted the very first World Cup. Thirteen teams travelled there, some of them for weeks by ship.
The first World Cup goal was scored by a Frenchman, Lucien Laurent.
In the final, Uruguay were losing to Argentina at half time.
They came back to win four two. The next day was declared a national holiday.

## 8 Ending
So, who invented football?
Nobody, and everybody.
Chinese soldiers kicking feathered balls. Greek athletes shouting across a line.
Japanese nobles keeping a ball in the air. Medieval villages wrecking their own market squares.
Schoolboys arguing about shins. And a group of men in a London tavern who finally wrote it all down.
Today, FIFA has 211 member associations. More countries than the United Nations.
The game that a king tried to ban now belongs to the whole world.
