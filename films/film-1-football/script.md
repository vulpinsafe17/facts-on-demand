# Where Did Football Really Come From? — voice-over script (EN)

In 1314, the King of England tried to ban a game. It didn't work. Neither did the next thirty bans. Today, that game is played by over 250 million people. And yet, nobody can agree on who actually invented it. So let's settle it. Where did football really come from?

Our story starts in China, more than two thousand years ago. In the city of Linzi, people were obsessed with a game called cuju. It literally means "kick ball". Simple name. Simple idea.
The ball? Two pieces of leather, stitched together and stuffed with feathers. Not exactly a World Cup match ball.
But the emperors loved it. Emperor Gaozu built a cuju court right at his palace. Generals used it to train their soldiers. Later, there were cuju clubs, touring teams, even professional players. Centuries before anyone had heard of a transfer window.
Then a Ming emperor banned it. And the punishment could include losing a foot. Which, for a football player, is a very serious red card.
But here's the twist. There's no evidence that cuju shaped the football we play today. To find that, we need to look somewhere else.

The ancient Greeks had a ball game called episkyros. But they mostly carried and threw the ball. So that's less football, more rugby.
In Japan, nobles played kemari, working together to keep a deerskin ball in the air. No winners. No losers. Very polite. Very un-football.

So let's jump to medieval England. Picture this. One village against the next. Any number of players. One ball. Almost no rules. They called it mob football, and it was exactly as chaotic as it sounds.
Then came April 13th, 1314. London merchants had had enough of the noise, and King Edward the Second banned football in the city.
Did it work? Of course not. His son Edward the Third banned it too. He wanted men practising archery for war, not chasing a ball. Between 1314 and 1667, England passed more than thirty laws against football.
Football won every single time.

But there was still a huge problem. Every school in England played by its own rules. In some, you could catch the ball. In others, you could kick your opponent in the shins. Yes, really.
In 1848, students at Cambridge tried to write one set of rules for everyone. In 1857, a group of cricket players in Sheffield wanted to stay fit through the winter. So they founded Sheffield Football Club, recognised by FIFA as the oldest football club in the world.

And then, on a Monday evening in October 1863, everything changed.
A solicitor named Ebenezer Cobb Morley thought football needed rules, just like cricket had. So clubs met at the Freemasons' Tavern in London. That night, they founded the Football Association.
The biggest fight was about something called hacking. Kicking your opponent in the leg. One club, Blackheath, really wanted to keep it.
They lost. In December 1863, the first rules were approved, with no hacking and no carrying the ball. Some clubs walked out to keep playing their way. That's the moment football and rugby officially went separate ways.

From there, football spread fast. In 1872, Scotland and England played the first ever international match. Four thousand fans watched. The final score?
Nil-nil. Some traditions start early.
In 1904, seven nations founded FIFA in Paris. And in 1930, Uruguay hosted the very first World Cup.

So, who invented football? Nobody, and everybody. Chinese soldiers, Greek athletes, Japanese nobles, rowdy medieval villages, and a group of men in a London tavern who finally wrote it all down.
The game that a king tried to ban now belongs to the whole world.
